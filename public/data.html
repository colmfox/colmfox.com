  • Special issue

    Youth Activism and Protest in Indonesia

    Co-edited with Edward Aspinall and Yatun Sastramidjaja. Under review at Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde (BKI).

    Summary of project: In this special issue we bring together new research on young activists in Indonesia. My co-editors and I worked with the authors at a workshop in Indonesia and through several rounds of revision. The articles include studies of emerging youth groups in West Papua’s secessionist movement, which combine digital media and filmmaking with ecological, socialist and feminist ideas; of the kajian, or written policy analyses, that students produced during the 2019–2020 protests, as a sign of their commitment to pluralist democracy; and of how student journalists exposed sexual violence on campus and pushed the government to issue new rules for universities.

    See also the edited volume Youth Activism in Asia: A New Wave (University of Michigan Press, 2026), free to read online.

  • Working paper

    Press Coverage and the Legitimization of Protest in Hong Kong and Thailand

    Earlier version presented at the APSA Annual Meeting, Philadelphia, 2024.

    Summary: The 2019–2021 pro-democracy protests in Hong Kong and Thailand drew worldwide media attention, and in this paper I ask whether the press legitimized or marginalized them. Studies of the “protest paradigm” find that the media tend to portray protesters as violent rather than as actors with legitimate goals, but most of this work looks at Western democracies. Using AI-assisted coding of domestic and foreign newspaper coverage, I find that the press was largely supportive of both movements. Coverage became more critical when demands grew more radical and protests more violent, in countries that restrict press freedom, and in outlets aligned with the ruling regime.

  • Working paper

    The Personalization of Politics in Southeast Asian Elections

    To be presented at the AAS Annual Conference, Boston, March 2027.

    Abstract: Scholars increasingly argue that politics is becoming more personalized, with news coverage focusing on leaders rather than parties. This matters because leader-centered politics can bring electoral volatility, weaker checks and balances, and a greater risk of personalist rule. Yet most research focuses on Western democracies. This paper presents the first large-scale study of personalization in Southeast Asia.

    Using AI-assisted content analysis of thousands of election articles from multiple newspapers in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand, it measures how prominent leaders are relative to parties. Media-based accounts expect coverage to become more personalized as the media change. But although internet use soared, leaders’ share of election coverage stayed largely flat in most countries.

    Instead, three political factors drive personalization. First, electoral rules: when a presidential race coincides with a legislative election, coverage shifts sharply toward leaders. Second, parties: personal vehicles and small parties are covered mainly through their leaders, while strong party organization increases coverage of parties and leaders alike. Third, leaders: those in national office attract coverage in their own right. Personalization, in short, is driven by politics, not the media.

  • Working paper

    The Personalization of Politics in Thailand: What 30,000 Election Posters Can Tell Us

    Builds on my 2018 article in Comparative Political Studies and my Thai election poster data.

    Summary of project: In this paper I build on my earlier study of more than 12,000 posters from Thailand’s 2011 election (Comparative Political Studies, 2018). That study found that campaigns for party-list seats were more national and those for constituency seats more local, and that large parties ran policy-focused campaigns while small parties relied on their leader’s image. For the 2023 election, in which the youth-oriented Move Forward Party unexpectedly won the most seats and votes, I photographed more than 18,000 posters, returning to the same regions and streets as in 2011. Comparing the two collections, twelve years apart, will show what has changed in campaign messages and what has stayed the same. I will ask whether any changes reflect the rise of Move Forward, the 2014 coup and nearly a decade of government under its leader, Prayut Chan-o-cha, Pheu Thai’s fall from a majority in 2011 to second place in 2023, or the growth of online campaigning. Both elections used two ballots, one for a local candidate and one for a national party list, so local and national campaigning can be compared in the same way in each year.

    The posters are part of the Thai election poster dataset.

Datasets and publications

Indonesian Election Posters

CodebookAbout the data

Between 2009 and 2012 I photographed over 25,000 election posters from the back of a motorbike, in legislative and regional head elections across most of Indonesia’s provinces. Each poster is in a database, coded for its content and its appeals to voters, including to ethnic and religious groups. The collection is the main source for my book, Bonding, Bridging, & Bypassing (Oxford University Press, 2024).

  • 2024 Bonding, Bridging, & Bypassing: Understanding Ethnic Politics in Diverse Societies Oxford University Press.
    BookSupplementaryReplication data
  • 2023 Ethnic Campaign Appeals: To Bond, Bridge, or Bypass? Political Communication 40(1): 92–114.
    JournalAccepted manuscriptSupplementaryReplication data

    In a time of rising ethno-nationalist politics and polarization, efforts to understand why some electoral candidates choose to politicize ethnicity while others do not have become increasingly urgent. One factor inhibiting our understanding in this area is the paucity of systematic measures of ethnic appeals. To help in rectifying this gap, I present an approach to identifying and measuring different types of ethnic campaign appeals – termed bonding and bridging appeals, respectively. I then apply that approach to an original dataset of over 1,500 election posters from almost 250 candidates competing in Indonesian elections. The findings indicate that candidates made ethnic bonding appeals if their ethnic group was politically viable in terms of both size and the lack of social constraints on appealing to that group. Meanwhile, candidates from nonviable groups bridged across ethnic groups or bypassed ethnicity entirely. When candidates belonged to two ethnic categories, they used the simple heuristic that bonding trumps bridging to guide their appeal strategy. These findings illustrate the importance of looking beyond ethnic demographics and also casts doubt on the broadly accepted expectation that candidates will appeal to the ethnic group that is of minimum winning size. Ultimately, the article helps us understand candidates' communication strategies and why ethnic diversity actually results in less exclusivist ethnic politics.

  • 2023 Islamic Political Parties and Election Campaigns in Indonesia With Jeremy Menchik. Party Politics 29(4): 622–635.
    JournalAccepted manuscriptSupplementaryReplication data

    Islamist political parties are a structural feature of politics across the Muslim world, raising persisting questions for scholars of democracy. Under what conditions will Islamists moderate to support democracy and pluralism? Under what conditions will they adopt more exclusive behavior? Taking a fresh approach, we focus on electoral competition and the conditions under which Islamic party candidates campaign using either inclusive nationalist appeals or exclusively Islamic appeals. Using a unique data source, we coded the appeals contained on the campaign posters of 572 Islamic party candidates in Indonesia. We found that demographics, urban-rural differences, and the level of government office (i.e., national or regional) affected the inclusive or exclusive nature of campaigns. We also highlight differences in appeals made by candidates from Muslim democratic and Islamist parties. The study illustrates the effectiveness of posters as a data source and presents a new approach to understanding the behavior of Islamic parties.

  • 2022 Visualizing Politics in Indonesia: The Design and Distribution of Election Posters International Journal of Communication 16: 4187–4209.
    Journal (open access)SupplementaryReplication data

    Where studies have shown that visuals are the primary means of political communication, research continues to focus largely on text-based information. To add to our understanding of visual-political communications, this article analyses Indonesian election posters since the 1950s. Drawing on historical materials and on a content analysis of 4,000 election posters, it asks why election posters have been designed and distributed in particular ways. Findings indicate that in the past, posters used singular, though powerful, social symbols to mobilize demographic groups behind political parties. However, contemporary posters are more visually complex and more candidate-centered, making arguments as to what the candidates represent. Furthermore, although the wide distribution of posters has always been used to signify strength, the number of posters has proliferated in recent elections. These trends can be explained by underlying social forces, advances in technology, institutional reforms, and the identities and types of parties and candidates.

Thai Election Posters

CodebookAbout the data

I photographed over 12,000 posters across Thailand in the 2011 election and over 18,000 more in 2023, returning to the same regions and streets. The 2011 posters are coded for how much of each poster goes to images and text, which shows whether a campaign is pitched locally or nationally; coding of the 2023 posters is under way. Together they will show what has changed in Thai campaigning, and what has stayed the same.

  • 2018 Is All Politics Local? Determinants of Local and National Election Campaigns Comparative Political Studies 51(14): 1899–1934.
    JournalAccepted manuscriptSupplementary ASupplementary BReplication data

    In recent decades election campaigns have shifted their focus from the local to the national level, increasingly featuring party leaders, labels, and national platforms. Despite this trend, there remains significant variation in the local/national orientation of campaigns across countries and parties. This article tests several propositions on why campaigns adopt a local or national orientation by analyzing a unique collection of more than 12,000 geocoded Thai election posters. Specialized software was used to measure the spatial proportions of visual and textual content on each poster. Using Thailand's mixed electoral system to enable a controlled comparison of electoral rules, I demonstrate that proportional rules were associated with national campaign strategies whereas majoritarian rules fostered local strategies. In addition, large parties ran party-centered, policy-focused campaigns whereas small parties relied more on their leader's image. This contrasts with Western countries, where large parties increasingly promote their leader's image and small parties emphasize narrower policy objectives.

Indonesian Press Campaign Reports

CodebookAbout the data

To trace how campaigns changed as Indonesia democratized, I coded campaign reports from the regional newspaper Waspada (Medan, North Sumatra) across the five legislative elections from 1997 to 2014, for campaign events, endorsements, and appeals to social groups. The reports show when and how candidates bring ethnicity into their campaigns, and are also used in Bonding, Bridging, & Bypassing.

  • 2024 Bonding, Bridging, & Bypassing: Understanding Ethnic Politics in Diverse Societies Oxford University Press.
    BookSupplementaryReplication data
  • 2018 Candidate-Centric Systems and the Politicization of Ethnicity: Evidence from Indonesia Democratization 25(7): 1190–1209.
    JournalAccepted manuscriptSupplementaryReplication data

    When and why do electoral candidates politicize ethnicity? From the literature, we might expect this behaviour to occur during democratic transitions or under proportional rules. However, empirical support for these arguments is mixed. This article presents a new approach, arguing that candidate-centric rules offer candidates incentives to politicize ethnicity. The argument is tested in Indonesia with empirical evidence drawn from coding newspaper reports on campaign events, endorsements and group appeals. Indonesia used party-centric rules from 1997 to 2004, and even though the country democratized during this period, the politicization of ethnicity actually declined. I show how party-centric rules, coupled with a national economic crisis, encouraged candidates to campaign on broad national platforms of reform and development, thereby appealing to the poor rather than to ethnic groups. Between 2004 and 2009, the system became more candidate-centric and the politicization of ethnicity increased. I argue that changes in the system freed candidates from national party platforms and motivated them to campaign on their local connections with ethnic groups. This study is particularly pertinent amidst the push for direct candidate-centric elections in the developing world and the lack of literature on how such rules could affect ethnic politics.

Indonesian Ethnic and Religious Coalitions

About the data

With Edward Aspinall, I collected an extensive record of ethnic and religious coalitions in Indonesian regional head elections: 675 candidate tickets in 123 elections across seven provinces, 2010–2013. We combined it with a survey of over 500 politicians and interviews with campaign workers to explain why candidates from different groups join forces, and why many build broader coalitions than they need to win.

  • Forth. Beyond Demographics: Ethnic and Religious Coalition Formation in Indonesia With Edward Aspinall. Perspectives on Politics.
    Replication data

    Existing research on intergroup coalition formation in diverse democracies emphasizes demographic calculations. The most influential approach predicts that candidates will form “minimum winning coalitions” just large enough to secure victory while minimizing their patronage obligations. To address this topic, we collected a unique dataset on interreligious and interethnic coalition formation by almost 700 Indonesian candidates competing in 123 local elections, surveyed over 500 politicians, and interviewed campaign workers. We found that intergroup coalition formation is explained by two distinct strategic logics. The first is size-based: candidates from smaller groups form intergroup coalitions to assemble a winning vote share. The second is status-based: candidates from groups that are vulnerable in national or regional hierarchies form coalitions with dominant groups in order to signal legitimacy and shield against backlash. Crucially, this status logic operates even when candidates from vulnerable groups already have local winning majorities. Finally, we found that candidates tend to maximize rather than minimize their intergroup coalition size, with scant evidence that candidates form minimum winning coalitions.

Covid-19 Editorials in East Asia

CodebookAbout the data

To see how the press in East Asia responded to the pandemic, I measured how much coverage English-language newspapers gave to Covid-19 in the first six months of 2020 and coded the tone and content of 330 editorials, from China (Global Times, China Daily), Hong Kong (South China Morning Post), Taiwan (Taipei Times), and South Korea (Korea Herald, Korea Times).

  • 2021 Media in a Time of Crisis: Newspaper Coverage of Covid-19 in East Asia Journalism Studies 22(13): 1853–1873.
    JournalAccepted manuscriptReplication data

    How have newspapers covered Covid-19 in Asia? To answer this question, I studied East Asian English-language newspapers published between January and July 2020. First, I measured the level of news media attention on Covid-19 among all reports. Second, I analyzed the tone and content of 330 editorials. I divided the analysis into two time periods: the initial crisis breakout period, when the number of infections was rising or high, and the crisis abatement period, when new infections declined to manageable levels. Findings show that although newspapers were slow to begin addressing the pandemic, their early editorials carried an alarming tone, which continued even after new infections dropped to low levels. This surprising level of concern continued because the topics shifted from health concerns to more ideological goals. Chinese and Taiwanese editors politicized the pandemic, using it as a wedge issue to attack international adversaries. Meanwhile, Korean editors used the economic fallout of the pandemic to press the government for pro-business economic reforms. In contrast, editors in Hong Kong exhibited cautious neutrality, largely avoiding politicization of the pandemic. These patterns of editorial coverage reveal the partisan nature of the press in East Asia, as well as salient political and economic undercurrents.
